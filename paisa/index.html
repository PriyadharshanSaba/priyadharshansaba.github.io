Paisa

A Build Log · Oct 2026

Paisa reads the emails my banks already send and tells me what I actually spent this month, without counting money I lent to friends, moved between my own accounts or paid on a card bill.

source: private repo, on request Email Portfolio

~$ How it looks

# a web app built for the phone first; it installs to the home screen

~$ What it does

  • Fills itself in. Payments arrive from the alert emails and statements banks already send, so there is almost nothing to type.
  • Counts only real spending. Money lent to a friend, moved between my own accounts or used to pay off a card is kept, but never added to what I spent.
  • Asks instead of guessing. Anything it does not recognise waits for me to tag it once, and next time it remembers.
  • Shows what the money is for. Savings, investments and goals sit on one bar, with a timeline of when each policy or account pays out.

~$ Why I built it

TODO · draft, rewrite in your words

I wanted one honest answer to "how much did I spend this month?" Banking apps answer a different question: how much left the account.

When I sorted one real month by hand, the gap was big. A loan to a friend, money passed on to someone else and other people's share of a group dinner made the month look about two-thirds more expensive than it was.

So Paisa reads the emails my banks already send, counts only what was really spent, and asks me rather than guessing. It is built for one person, me, and runs on my own hosting.

# key decisions

Money is integer paise

Every amount is an INTEGER of paise (BIGINT on Postgres), never a float. Parsing goes through Decimal with explicit ROUND_HALF_UP, in one module. A float round-trip turns 470.82 into 470.81999…, and a month's total drifts. Indian lakh/crore grouping is done by hand, not with locale, and typed amounts like "18L" or "40k" are multiplied as exact decimals.

kind decides what counts as spending

Each row has a kind: spend, invest, lent, repaid or transfer. Only spend is summed as living costs. A card bill payment is a transfer, so a purchase is never counted twice, and a split bill becomes two rows sharing a split_group: my share as spend, the rest as lent.

One source of truth per account

A phone payment shows up in the payment app and in the bank's alert email. Each account names the one source allowed to write its rows. Messages from any other source are dropped and recorded in email_seen with the reason, so nothing disappears silently.

Three kinds of duplicate

A partial unique index on the bank reference (UTR) makes the database reject a second copy of the same payment. A re-import of the same row is skipped. A same-amount row within ±1 day from another source is flagged maybe_dupe_of for review, never merged by guess.

Idempotent sync, two-step import

The Gmail cursor moves only when a sync finishes. A sync that dies halfway replays the range next time, and the unique message ids make the replay a no-op. A pasted statement import is two endpoints: /import/preview writes nothing, /import/commit writes.

Statements must add up

A statement parser checks its lines against the statement's own totals and balances. If they don't reconcile, nothing is written.

No silent guesses

An unmatched payment lands with needs_review = 1 and no category. A rule may file a payment by itself only up to ₹2,000. Above that the match is a suggestion, and emailed rows are never auto-filed.

Two databases, one SQL

Queries are written once in SQLite style; the Postgres connection translates placeholders. Every migration exists twice. Inserts that may fail run inside a SAVEPOINT, because Postgres aborts the whole transaction otherwise. Tests always get a SQLite file and can never reach production.

A private mode for demos

One toggle masks every amount, person and account number as ****, and makes the app read-only. The masking filters are context-aware, so Jinja can't fold a literal at compile time and leak it. A sweep test renders every page in private mode and checks that no amount or name is left.

Tested against real data

Parser fixtures are real bank emails and statements, anonymised. A real statement must parse to an exact row count and paise total, checked against the bank by hand. If a change moves that number, the parser is wrong, not the fixture.
